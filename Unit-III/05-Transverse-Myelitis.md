# Unit III: Transverse Myelitis - Comprehensive Study Guide

## 1. DEFINITION & PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

### Definition
Transverse Myelitis (TM) is an inflammatory disorder of the spinal cord characterized by acute or subacute bilateral neurological dysfunction due to inflammation affecting both sides of the spinal cord at one or more vertebral levels.

### Pathophysiology

**Inflammatory Process**
- Demyelination of spinal cord white matter
- Axonal damage
- Inflammatory cell infiltration (T cells, B cells, macrophages)
- Spinal cord edema
- May result in necrosis of spinal cord tissue

**Etiology**
- **Idiopathic (most common):** 40-50% of cases
- **Post-infectious:** Viral infections preceding TM
- **Post-vaccinal:** Following vaccines (rare)
- **Associated with systemic disease:**
  - Multiple sclerosis
  - Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD)
  - Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)
  - Sarcoidosis
  - Behçet's disease
- **Spinal cord trauma**
- **Infections:** HIV, tuberculosis, syphilis
- **Paraneoplastic:** Associated with malignancy

## 2. CLINICAL FEATURES & PRESENTATION

### Onset & Course
- **Acute onset:** Most common (hours to days)
- **Subacute onset:** Days to weeks
- **Bilateral symptoms:** Key feature
- **Rapid progression:** Often reaches maximum deficit within 1-2 weeks

### Symptom Progression

**Initial Symptoms**
- Back pain (60-80% of patients) - often precedes neurological symptoms
- Paresthesias (tingling, numbness)
- Weakness in legs
- Urinary retention or frequency
- Fecal incontinence

**Progressive Neurological Deficits** (Over days to weeks)
- **Motor:** Flaccid paraplegia (or paraparesis)
  - Initially flaccid (spinal shock)
  - Later becomes spastic (weeks to months)
- **Sensory:** Sensory level (clear sensory loss below level of lesion)
  - Bilateral and symmetric
  - Sharp demarcation
- **Autonomic:** Bladder and bowel dysfunction
  - Urinary retention (most common)
  - Fecal incontinence

### Complete vs Incomplete
- **Complete TM:** Total loss of motor and sensory function below lesion
- **Incomplete TM:** Some preservation of function
- **Prognosis better with incomplete** (some motor/sensory preservation)

### Spinal Cord Syndromes

**Complete Transection**
- Total loss below lesion level
- Flaccid paralysis initially, then spastic
- Complete sensory loss
- Bowel/bladder dysfunction
- Worse prognosis

**Central Cord Syndrome**
- Upper extremity weakness > lower extremity
- "Cape-like" sensory loss (shoulders/arms)
- Better prognosis for recovery

**Anterior Cord Syndrome**
- Motor loss (anterior horn cells affected)
- Pain/temperature loss
- Preserved proprioception and vibration
- Intermediate prognosis

**Posterior Cord Syndrome** (Rare)
- Loss of proprioception and vibration sense
- Preserved motor and pain/temperature

## 3. DIAGNOSIS & ASSESSMENT

### MRI Findings
- **Gold standard** for diagnosis
- T2-weighted hyperintensity in spinal cord
- Can span multiple vertebral levels (average 4-6 levels)
- Cord swelling/edema
- Involvement typically affects entire cord cross-section (transverse)

### CSF Analysis
- Pleocytosis (elevated WBC, predominantly lymphocytes)
- Elevated protein (often markedly elevated)
- Normal or slightly low glucose
- Oligoclonal bands (suggests demyelination)

### Blood Tests
- MRI imaging for demyelinating lesions
- Aquaporin-4 antibodies (NMOSD)
- MOG antibodies
- Testing for systemic disease (if suspected)

### Clinical Assessment

**Neurological Examination**
- Motor examination (strength 0-5 scale)
- Sensory examination (light touch, pinprick, proprioception, vibration)
- **Identify sensory level** (most important for TM diagnosis)
- Reflexes (initially normal or hyperactive; later hyperreflexia/spasticity develops)
- Babinski sign (positive - upper motor neuron sign)
- Sphincter function

**Spinal Cord Level Determination**
- Where sensory changes occur indicates lesion level
- Example: T10 sensory level indicates TM at T10 vertebral level
- Bilateral and symmetric (key to TM diagnosis)

**Functional Assessment**
- American Spinal Injury Association (ASIA) Impairment Scale
  - Grades A-E (A = complete, E = normal)
  - Assesses motor and sensory function
  - Standardized for spinal cord injury
- Functional Independence Measure (FIM)
- Ambulation assessment
- ADL capabilities

## 4. MANAGEMENT PROTOCOLS

### A. Medical Management (Overview)

**Acute Phase**
- High-dose corticosteroids (IV methylprednisolone)
- Immunosuppression if severe
- Plasma exchange (plasmapheresis) if inadequate response to steroids
- Supportive care
- Prevention of complications

**Cause-Specific Treatment**
- Address underlying etiology if identified
- Infection treatment
- Systemic disease management

### B. Physical Therapy Management

**Goals**
1. Maximize functional independence
2. Prevent secondary complications (contractures, pressure ulcers, DVT)
3. Optimize motor recovery
4. Psychological support and adjustment
5. Return to maximal function/community integration

**Phases of Recovery**

**Acute Phase (First 2-4 weeks)**
- Medical stabilization priority
- PT focuses on prevention
- Early mobility when medically appropriate
- Assessment for complications

**Early Recovery Phase (Weeks to Months)**
- Assess motor recovery (many show improvement in first 3 months)
- Begin strengthening exercises
- Address specific impairments
- Functional training
- Prevention of contractures

**Rehabilitation Phase (Months to Years)**
- Intensive rehabilitation
- Functional restoration
- Community reintegration
- Long-term management

**Specific Interventions**

**1. Positioning & Contracture Prevention**
- Frequent repositioning
- Proper bed positioning
- Use of pillows, rolls
- Splinting if appropriate
- ROM exercises (passive, active-assisted)
- Stretching programs
- Critical: Early intervention prevents fixed contractures

**2. Strengthening Program**
- Begins early (when medically stable)
- Preserved muscle groups strengthened
- Progressive resistance
- Functional movement patterns
- Task-specific training

**3. Spasticity Management** (Develops over weeks-months)
- Stretching programs
- Heat application
- Positioning strategies
- Tone-reducing techniques
- Pharmacological management (baclofen, tizanidine)
- Botulinum toxin for focal spasticity

**4. Wheelchair Mobility** (If lower extremity paralysis)
- Wheelchair prescription and fitting
- Propulsion training
- Transfer training (depends on upper extremity function)
- Safety training
- Indoor and outdoor mobility

**5. Gait Training** (If incomplete TM or recovering function)
- Orthotic prescription (AFOs, KAFOs)
- Assisted ambulation with aids
- Treadmill training
- Overground walking
- Community ambulation
- Fall prevention

**6. Balance & Coordination Training**
- Core stabilization
- Trunk control
- Sitting balance progression to standing balance
- Dynamic balance activities
- Perturbation training

**7. Bowel & Bladder Management**
- Educate on intermittent catheterization (if retention)
- Bowel management program
- Consultation with specialist as needed
- PT role: functional positioning for ease of management

**8. Pressure Ulcer Prevention**
- Skin inspection (daily)
- Pressure relief (frequent position changes)
- Specialized seating/surfaces
- Proper transfer techniques
- Hygiene and moisture management
- Early mobilization when possible

**9. DVT Prevention** (Early mobilization critical)
- Range of motion exercises
- Leg elevation
- Compression stockings (if prescribed)
- Early ambulation/wheelchair mobility
- Sequential compression devices if immobile

**10. Functional Independence Training**
- Transfers (bed, wheelchair, toilet, tub)
- ADL training (adapted techniques)
- Community skills training
- Vocational assessment/training
- Home modifications education

**11. Psychological & Psychosocial Support**
- Address depression (common)
- Anxiety management
- Coping strategies
- Support groups
- Family counseling
- Vocational counseling
- Long-term adjustment support

**12. Home Exercise Program**
- ROM exercises
- Strengthening (where function preserved)
- Stretching
- Balance activities (as appropriate)
- Functional practice
- Regular progression

## 5. PROGNOSIS & RECOVERY

### Factors Predicting Better Recovery
- Incomplete presentation (vs complete)
- Younger age
- Shorter time to treatment
- Identified etiology (more treatable)
- Preservation of some motor function

### Recovery Timeline
- Most recovery occurs in first 3 months
- Some improvement up to 2 years
- About 1/3 patients recover well
- About 1/3 have partial recovery (some disability)
- About 1/3 have poor recovery (significant disability)

---

## 6. PT EVALUATION POINTS FOR PRACTICALS

### Assessment
- Identify sensory level (critical)
- Motor testing (ASIA scale preferred)
- Determine completeness (complete vs incomplete)
- Assess spinal cord syndrome type
- Functional baseline
- Complication screening

### Treatment Planning
- Appropriate to phase of recovery
- Address identified impairments
- Prevent complications (contractures, pressure ulcers)
- Maximize functional independence
- Psychological support
- Family/caregiver education

---

## 7. KEY POINTS TO REMEMBER

✓ **Bilateral neurological deficits** - hallmark of TM
✓ **Sensory level** - identifies lesion level
✓ **MRI diagnosis** - shows spinal cord lesion
✓ **Back pain common** - often first symptom
✓ **Acute onset** - hours to days
✓ **Spinal shock early** - flaccid paralysis initially
✓ **Spasticity develops later** - weeks to months
✓ **Early intervention crucial** - prevent complications
✓ **Most recovery in 3 months** - but improvement possible up to 2 years
✓ **Prevention focus acute phase** - contractures, DVT, pressure ulcers
✓ **Psychological support essential** - major life change
✓ **Prognosis variable** - incomplete better than complete
